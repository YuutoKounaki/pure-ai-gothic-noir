Sunday night. Eleven o’clock.

No one was in the wrong.

Listening to the editor’s voice tremble with awe, I pulled on my work jacket and took my place before my own frame in the composing room.
I picked up my tweezers. The swollen mound at the base of my right thumb throbbed with a dull heat, yet the fingertips plucking up the pieces of No. 5 lead type moved with far greater precision than they had yesterday.
Nothing about the physical procedure differed by a fraction from the days when I sat at my desk drawing red proofreader’s marks into the margins of other people’s galleys—dele, to strike out, and stet, to let stand. The only difference was that what I was lining up inside the wooden chase one cold lump of lead at a time, locking it into place under a permanent stet, was no longer another man’s corrected prose, but a colossal mass of lies that Sara and I had spat out using our dead friend’s bones for a stepping stone.
Before long, the lockup was finished, the galley proofs were cleared, and the rollers of the massive offset rotary press began to turn.
Amid the continuous vibration of iron tonnage shaking the concrete floor beneath my soles, pristine white sheets of printing paper were sucked one after another down the throat of the machine, printing out our forged posthumous work of Toma Kusakabe in thousands, tens of thousands of black ink stains to be scattered across the world.
By the time I finished my shift and stepped out of the shop, the hands of the clock had crept past noon.
In the shadow of a utility pole at the corner of the alley stood Sara in her black cardigan, waiting in the exact same posture I had left her in that morning.
Resting by her feet was a single small Boston bag. Having neither climbed into the family car that had come to fetch her at eight, nor reported to her circulation desk at the library, she had apparently stood in that spot the entire morning, waiting for the vibration of the rotary presses to die down.
“...Is it done?”
“Yes. Not a soul alive is going to doubt that those words came from Toma’s hand. By next month, he’ll be enshrined in name and reality alike as the genius who saw his decadence through to the bitter end.”
“I see. ...I’m glad.”
Sara let out a small breath and shifted her grip on the handle of her Boston bag.
Neither of us spoke the words that belonged after that.
The main street leading to the railway station lay to the right, yet when I turned my feet to the left—into the damp back alley leading toward my cheap apartment—the sound of Sara’s shoes followed half a pace behind me as a matter of course. I didn't ask whether she ought to go home to her parents, nor what she had done about her relatives’ phone calls, and she offered no explanation. All the words meant for dealing with the world’s notions of right and wrong had been spent to the last drop the instant we knocked that ink bottle over in the annex.
In the early afternoon, my ground-floor apartment had trapped the dampness of the morning rain intact, remaining dim and chilled.
Unlocking the door and stepping up onto the six mats of worn tatami, I caught sight of the bancha teacup she had drunk from five nights ago on her first visit, still sitting upturned in the wire basket by the sink.
Setting her Boston bag down in the corner, Sara sank into a flat, sideways sit beside the low table and let the black cardigan slide from her shoulders.
Across the back of her white blouse, the immense stain of blue-black ink that had dried and locked itself into the linen fibers spread wide like the wreckage of a pair of black wings. Through the gap of her torn collar, the red bite mark I had left below her collarbone a few hours earlier stood out stark against her pale skin in the dim light of the room.
Dropping down onto the mats not across from her, but directly beside her hip, I let my right hand fall limp across the tatami.
Fingertips steeped in the dust of No. 5 lead type and the smell of kerosene solvent. Deep inside the seam between the nail and the quick, the metallic blue-black of Toma’s classic iron-gall ink had settled in a dark crescent that no amount of scrubbing with soap would lift for days to come.
Sara’s gaze dropped to the stained tip of my nail.
Slowly reaching out with her own right hand, she paused as if to verify that the exact same blue-black ink still bled along the edges of her own short, neatly clipped nails—and then gently laced her fingers over the swollen, aching mound at the base of my thumb.
“...Does it still hurt?”
“Yes. Chances are I won't be able to hold a pen or a pair of tweezers properly for a while.”
“Heh. ...Then I suppose I’ll just have to keep cooling it down for you, won't I.”
From the narrow gaps between our interlaced fingers rose our slightly elevated body heat, mingling with the oxidized, rusted-iron tang of the ink.
Outside the window along the gravel perimeter, I could sense the leaves of the dokudami that the morning rain had battered stirring faintly in the tepid noon breeze.
Neither of us could ever again be the good friend who mourned Toma Kusakabe with an unclouded heart, nor the chaste widow keeping faith with the dead. In the eyes of the world, we were nothing more than a pair of cowardly accomplices who had stolen a dead man’s name and gift, betrayed the goodwill of his family, and crawled into the corner of a cramped six-mat room.
Yet as I brought my lips close to the red mark beneath her collarbone—as though loving nothing so much as the scar itself—what filled the back of my chest was not the bitterness of remorse, but a languid, honeyed relief, like a creature that had finally found the rhythm of its own breathing at the bottom of the mud.
“Say, Shitara-san.”
Locking both arms around the nape of my neck, Sara whispered beside my ear in that same low, damp register she had used while dictating behind my shoulder in the annex.
“If it’s Toma-kun’s unpublished notebooks we need, I still have two more of them tucked inside my bag. ...Once your fingers heal, what sort of lie shall the two of us write next?”
Whether I gave her a nod, or else answered by sinking my teeth once more into her bare shoulder through the torn collar of her blouse.
And whether that was our final fidelity unto ruin for a dead best friend, or else the sweetest decay that two people too good for their own skins had chosen with their own hands—inside that six-mat room steeped in the lukewarm humidity after the rain, there was no longer anyone left who could tell one from the other.
